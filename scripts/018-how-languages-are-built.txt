Here is a strange fact. A Japanese baby, at six months old, can hear the difference between an English "r" and an English "l" about as well as an American baby can. By the time that same child turns one, the ability has mostly faded. Nobody taught them to stop hearing it. Their brain simply decided, on the evidence of a year of listening, that the difference did not matter.

Now flip it around. An English-speaking adult trying to learn Hindi will struggle to hear the difference between two "t" sounds, one made with the tongue against the teeth and one made with the tongue curled back against the roof of the mouth. To a Hindi speaker, those are as different as "pat" and "bat." To you, they are the same sound said twice.

So when people say a language is "hard," what they usually mean is that it is far from the language they already have. Difficulty is not a property of a language. It is a distance between two languages. And once you see languages as systems with a small number of design choices, you can measure that distance, part by part, and plan around it.

That is what today is about. Here is the roadmap. First, sound: how every language picks a tiny set of distinctions out of everything a mouth can do, and why your ears get locked in. Second, words: the very different ways languages build them, from Chinese to Turkish to Mohawk. Third, grammar: word order, case, gender, and the surprising regularities that hold across thousands of unrelated languages. Fourth, the difficulty question itself, including the government's ranking of hard languages and what it does and does not tell you. Fifth, what the research says about how adults actually learn one, and which popular beliefs about that are wrong.

Let's start with sound.

Part one. Sound.

The human vocal tract can make hundreds of distinguishable sounds. No language uses most of them. Each one picks a set, usually somewhere between about twenty and about fifty, and treats those as its building blocks. Linguists call the building blocks phonemes. A phoneme is not really a sound. It is a category, a bucket of sounds that the language treats as the same.

Here is the clearest example. Say the word "pin" with your hand in front of your mouth. You feel a puff of air after the "p." Now say "spin." Much less air. Those are physically different sounds. But in English they are the same phoneme, and you have probably never noticed the difference in your life. In Thai or Hindi, that puff of air alone can change one word into another. The difference is real in both languages. Only one of them cares about it.

So learning the sound system of a language means learning where it draws its bucket boundaries. And here is the catch. Your brain already has buckets, and they are not neutral. The research on this goes back to the early nineteen eighties, when the psychologist Janet Werker tested infants on non-native sound contrasts. Babies around six to eight months could tell apart sounds from languages they had never heard. By ten to twelve months, most of that was gone for contrasts their own language did not use.

The later work of Patricia Kuhl added a striking mechanism. Each phoneme category has a best example, a prototype, and sounds near the prototype get pulled toward it in perception. She called it a perceptual magnet. The effect is that differences close to one of your own categories are squashed, and you literally hear two sounds as more similar than they are. This is why the Japanese listener struggles with "r" and "l". Japanese has one sound in that region, and both English sounds get pulled into it.

Is the window closed for good? No, and this is where the popular version oversimplifies. Adults can be trained to hear new contrasts. In lab studies, a few weeks of focused practice with feedback, especially with many different speakers saying the words, produces real and lasting improvement. It rarely reaches native level. But "babies can, adults can't" is too strong. The honest version is that adults start with a perceptual handicap that is expensive, but not impossible, to remove.

Tone is the extreme case. In Mandarin, the syllable "ma" means different things depending on whether the pitch is high and level, rising, dipping, or falling. That is four tones, plus a light neutral one. Cantonese has more. English uses pitch too, but for attitude and questions, not for word identity. So the English speaker has to repurpose a dimension their brain has filed under "emotion" into a dimension that carries dictionary meaning. That is not just a new bucket. It is a new axis.

Here is the practical lesson from part one. Pronunciation and listening are not decorations you add at the end. Your existing categories actively distort what you hear, and what you can't hear, you can't reliably say or remember. Early, deliberate work on the sound contrasts that matter in your target language pays off for everything downstream.

Part two. Words.

Once a language has sounds, it has to package meaning into words. And here the variety is enormous.

Linguists sort languages along a spectrum of how much meaning gets packed into a single word. At one end are isolating languages, like Mandarin or Vietnamese. Words tend to be short and rarely change form. There is no past tense ending on a verb. Instead you add a separate word, or let context do the work. "I go yesterday" is roughly how the grammar runs.

In the middle are agglutinative languages, like Turkish, Finnish, Korean, Japanese and Swahili. These build words by gluing on pieces, one after another, each with one job. Turkish is the textbook case. Take the word for house, "ev." Add "ler" and it's plural, houses. Add "im" and it's my houses. Add "de" and it's in my houses. Each piece is a clean, separable unit. You can get very long words, but they are like Lego. Once you know the pieces, the whole is predictable.

Then there are fusional languages, like Latin, Spanish, Russian, and the older stages of English. These also change word endings, but a single ending carries several meanings at once, fused together. In Spanish, the "o" at the end of "hablo" means first person, singular, present tense and indicative mood, all in one letter. You can't pull those meanings apart. That is why Spanish verb tables exist and why students memorize them: the pieces aren't separable, so you learn the whole grid.

At the far end are polysynthetic languages, such as Mohawk or Inuktitut, where a single long word can carry what English needs a whole sentence to say, with subject, object and verb all bundled in.

Here's the question that matters for learning. Which of these is "hard"? Each makes a different trade. Isolating languages have almost no endings to memorize but lean heavily on word order, small helper words and context. Agglutinative languages have lots of endings, but they are regular. Fusional languages have fewer pieces but more irregularity. English speakers tend to find fusional languages from their own family manageable, partly because they've met the pattern, and agglutinative ones intimidating at first, even though the system underneath is often more regular.

There is another layer to words, and it is easy to underestimate. Vocabulary is the single biggest cost in learning a language, and it is very unevenly distributed. Word frequency in every language follows a steep curve, often described by Zipf's law: the most common word shows up about twice as often as the second most common, about three times as often as the third, and so on down a long tail. The practical result is that a relatively small core of frequent words covers most of what you hear, and then each additional percent of coverage gets very expensive.

The applied linguist Paul Nation estimated that to read ordinary text comfortably, meaning you know about ninety-eight out of every hundred words, you need something like eight to nine thousand word families. For speech, somewhat fewer. Why ninety-eight percent and not ninety? Because at ninety percent, one word in ten is unknown. That is a mystery word in nearly every sentence, and you can't infer them from context because the context is full of holes too. Comprehension doesn't degrade gently. It falls off a cliff.

And here is the hidden reason Spanish is easier for an English speaker than Japanese. It isn't mainly grammar. After the Norman conquest of 1066, English absorbed a huge amount of French, and through French and later through scholarship, Latin. A large share of English vocabulary, especially the formal and technical part, has close relatives in Spanish, French and Italian. "Information" is "información." "Necessary" is "necesario." An English speaker starting Spanish already owns thousands of words in disguise. Starting Japanese, you own almost none, aside from some modern borrowings from English. That difference alone is worth many hundreds of hours.

Part three. Grammar.

Every language has to solve the same basic problem. In a sentence like "the dog bit the man," how do you mark who did what to whom?

There are two main strategies, and most languages use some mixture. One is word order. English leans on this heavily. "The dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" contain the same words, and only the order tells you which is news. The other strategy is case: marking the role of a noun with an ending or a small attached particle. In a language with rich case marking, the subject wears one ending and the object wears another, so you can shuffle the order around for emphasis and the meaning survives.

Old English, about a thousand years ago, had a working case system, something like German today. Over the following centuries, most of those endings wore away. We kept a fossil of it in our pronouns. "He" versus "him," "she" versus "her," "who" versus "whom." Those are case forms. Once the endings faded, English had to lock down its word order to keep roles clear. That's one reason English feels rigid about order and why learners of German, Russian or Finnish find case endings so foreign. Finnish has around fifteen cases. Russian has six, each with different endings depending on the noun's gender and number.

So what about word order across the world? This is one of the most interesting findings in linguistics. If you sort languages by the basic order of subject, object and verb, there are six logical possibilities. But they are not equally common. In the large database called the World Atlas of Language Structures, the two dominant orders are subject, object, verb, as in Japanese, Korean, Turkish and Hindi, and subject, verb, object, as in English, Chinese and Spanish. Each of those covers very roughly forty percent of the languages that have a dominant order. Verb first, as in Welsh or classical Arabic, is a distant third, under ten percent. Orders where the object comes before the subject are vanishingly rare.

That is surprising, and it gets more interesting. In the nineteen sixties, the linguist Joseph Greenberg noticed that word order choices travel in packs. If a language puts the verb at the end, it overwhelmingly tends to use postpositions, little words that come after the noun, like Japanese "Tokyo ni," "Tokyo to," instead of prepositions. It tends to put relative clauses before the noun they describe, and the possessor before the thing possessed. If a language puts the verb before the object, the reverse tends to hold. These are tendencies, not laws; there are exceptions. But they are strong.

Why would that be? The leading explanation is about processing. Roughly, a language is easier to parse if the "head" of each phrase sits consistently on the same side. A verb is the head of a verb phrase. A preposition is the head of its phrase. If heads go first everywhere, or last everywhere, the listener's parser can use one rule, and dependent pieces stay close to what they depend on. Languages that mix the patterns pay a small cost, and over many generations of speakers, small costs shape what survives.

Here's why this matters for a learner. Japanese doesn't just "put the verb at the end." It is, largely, English in a mirror. The verb comes last, the particles come after the nouns, the descriptive clause comes before the noun. "The book that I bought yesterday" comes out, word for word, as "yesterday I bought book." Once you see it as one consistent flip rather than fifty separate oddities, it becomes much less chaotic. It's one design choice propagated through the whole system.

Then there is grammatical gender, which English speakers find especially arbitrary. In German, the word for girl, "Mädchen," is grammatically neuter, because words ending in that diminutive suffix are neuter. Gender in these languages is mostly a noun class system, a set of agreement categories. It's only loosely tied to biological sex. Some languages, like Swahili, have many more noun classes, tied partly to meaning, such as people, trees, or abstract things. The learning cost of gender isn't the concept. It is that you must store a class tag with every noun, and get the agreement right on everything that touches it.

Before we leave grammar, a word on a famous idea: that the language you speak shapes how you think. This is usually called the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis. The strong version, that your language limits what you can think, has not held up. Speakers of languages without a future tense can plan for the future, and people without a word for a color can still see it. But a weaker version has some support. For instance, Russian has two basic color words for what English calls blue, a light blue and a dark blue, and in one well known experiment Russian speakers were slightly faster at telling apart shades that straddled that boundary. The effect was small, measured in fractions of a second, and it shrank when people had to perform a verbal task at the same time. So language nudges attention and habits at the margins. It does not wall off thought.

Part four. So which languages are hard?

The most cited ranking comes from the Foreign Service Institute, the US State Department's language school. They train diplomats, and they have decades of data on how long it takes native English speakers to reach professional working proficiency.

Their easiest group includes Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Dutch, Norwegian and Swedish, at roughly six to seven and a half months of full-time class, about six to seven hundred and fifty hours. German sits a little above that. Then come a large middle group, including Russian, Greek, Hindi, Turkish, Finnish and Hebrew, at about forty-four weeks. The hardest group, at about eighty-eight weeks, around twenty-two hundred hours, and usually with a year spent in the country, includes Arabic, Mandarin, Cantonese, Korean and Japanese, with Japanese often flagged as especially hard.

Before you take that as law, here is what it actually measures. These are intensive classes, for highly motivated adults, many of whom already speak other languages, aiming for a specific professional standard. The numbers are institutional averages, not a precise physical constant. But the ranking itself is quite stable, and you can now explain it from the parts we've covered.

Take Japanese. Sounds: actually fairly easy. Japanese has a small vowel inventory and simple syllables, and it has pitch accent but not full tones. Grammar: the mirror-image word order and the particles take getting used to, but it's regular. Politeness levels add real complexity: verbs change form depending on your social relationship to the listener. Vocabulary: almost no shared roots, so nearly every word is a fresh memorization cost. And then the writing system, which is the killer. Two syllable alphabets, plus the Chinese-derived characters called kanji, about two thousand of which are on the official list for general use, most with more than one reading depending on context.

Now Mandarin. Grammar: in many ways simpler than English. No verb conjugation, no plurals on nouns, no grammatical gender. Vocabulary: unrelated to English. Sound: tones, a whole new axis, plus some unfamiliar consonants. Writing: thousands of characters, which do not spell out pronunciation in any reliable way.

And Arabic: a different script, though an alphabet, so that is the lesser problem. Several sounds made deep in the throat that English doesn't use. Vocabulary built on a root system, where three consonants carry a core meaning and vowel patterns around them build related words. The root k, t, b relates to writing, and gives you words for book, writer, office, and library. That system is elegant once you see it, but unfamiliar at first. And the biggest practical problem: the formal written language differs substantially from the spoken dialects, so a learner often ends up learning, in effect, one and a half languages.

So the FSI ranking isn't about which languages are intrinsically complex. It's an additive cost over the components we've walked through: sounds outside your categories, vocabulary without shared roots, grammar organized on unfamiliar axes, and a writing system that doesn't map onto speech. Spanish is cheap on nearly every line. Japanese is expensive on several at once.

Part five. How adults actually learn one.

Let's start with age, because the belief here is strong and partly wrong. The usual claim is "you have to learn as a child." In 2018, Joshua Hartshorne, Joshua Tenenbaum and Steven Pinker published a large study using an online grammar quiz taken by about two thirds of a million English speakers, some native and some not. They modeled how the age of first exposure related to final grammatical ability. Their estimate was that the ability to learn grammar stays high well into the teenage years and only starts to decline around seventeen or eighteen. But because learning takes many years, to reach a level indistinguishable from a native speaker you probably need to start by about ten.

Researchers have debated the details of that model, and it only measured grammar, not accent or vocabulary. But the shape of the finding matters. It does not say adults can't learn. It says they can't easily become indistinguishable from natives. Those are very different goals. Adults actually learn faster than children in the early stages, because they bring better memory strategies, literacy and the ability to understand explicit explanations. Children win the long game mostly because they get thousands of hours of immersion with nothing else to do and no fear of sounding silly.

So what works? Here is where the field has argued for decades, and the arguments are worth knowing.

In the nineteen eighties, the linguist Stephen Krashen made a very influential claim, called the input hypothesis. Language is acquired, he said, by understanding messages, specifically by getting what he called comprehensible input, material slightly beyond your current level that you can still mostly understand. On his account, explicit grammar study and speaking practice contribute little to real acquisition.

The part of that which held up is the emphasis on huge amounts of understandable input. Reading and listening at the right level is the engine. The part that didn't hold up is the claim that it is sufficient. Merrill Swain studied Canadian French immersion students who had had years of excellent input. They understood French very well, but their speaking and writing stayed noticeably non-native in grammar. Her conclusion was that producing language forces you to notice gaps that comprehension lets you skip over. When you're listening, you can understand a sentence without processing every ending. When you're speaking, you have to choose one. And a large meta-analysis in 2000 by John Norris and Lourdes Ortega found that explicit instruction, actually being told the rule and practicing it, produced substantial and durable gains, which cuts against Krashen's strongest claim.

The modern consensus is roughly a combination. A large volume of comprehensible input is the base. Speaking and writing, with feedback, force you to process the grammar fully. And explicit instruction speeds things up, especially for features that are hard to notice on your own, like case endings that are barely pronounced, or contrasts your ear doesn't hear yet.

There are also two findings from memory research that apply directly. The first is the spacing effect. Going back to Hermann Ebbinghaus in the eighteen eighties, and replicated many times since, reviews spread out over time produce much more durable memory than the same number of reviews crammed together. This is the logic behind spaced repetition flashcard software, which schedules each item just before you're likely to forget it. For vocabulary, the biggest cost we identified, this is one of the most robust tools psychology has.

The second is the testing effect, sometimes called retrieval practice. Pulling something out of memory strengthens it more than looking at it again. Rereading a word list feels productive and isn't. Trying to recall the word, failing, and checking is uncomfortable and works.

Put all of this together and you can plan a language like a system. Front-load the sound contrasts your native categories will squash, because everything else depends on hearing correctly. Attack vocabulary in frequency order with spaced retrieval, because frequency coverage is steep and comprehension falls off a cliff below about ninety-eight percent. Learn the grammar as a small number of design choices, verb position, case, head direction, gender, rather than a pile of exceptions. Then flood yourself with input just beyond your level, and speak and write enough to expose what you only thought you knew.

Let's recap.

Languages are systems built from a few big choices. In sound, each language picks a small set of categories out of everything a mouth can do, and by your first birthday your brain has tuned itself to those categories, which squashes distinctions other languages rely on. Adults can retrain this, with effort, but it rarely becomes native-like.

In words, languages range from isolating, like Mandarin, through agglutinative, like Turkish, to fusional, like Spanish, and polysynthetic, like Mohawk. Each trades memorization against regularity differently. Vocabulary is the largest cost, frequency is steeply skewed, and you need around ninety-eight percent coverage before comprehension really works. English's huge Latin and French inheritance gives you a head start in Romance languages that has nothing to do with grammar.

In grammar, languages mark who did what to whom with word order, case, or both. English lost most of its case and locked its word order to compensate. Word order patterns cluster in ways Greenberg identified, likely because consistent head direction makes sentences easier to parse, which is why Japanese reads as English in a mirror rather than as chaos. The strong form of the idea that language limits thought hasn't held up; there are small effects on attention at most.

Difficulty is a distance, not a property. The Foreign Service Institute ranking from about six months to nearly two years of full-time study is explained by stacking costs: sounds, vocabulary roots, grammar axes and writing systems.

And for adults, the critical period claim is too strong. Grammar learning ability stays high until late adolescence, and adults learn faster early on; what's hard is reaching native-like. What works is lots of comprehensible input, plus output with feedback, plus explicit instruction for things you won't notice, all supported by spaced retrieval.

Now, five questions. Pause after each one and answer out loud before moving on.

Question one. A Japanese adult has trouble hearing the difference between English "r" and "l." Explain the mechanism. Why is it not just that the sounds are unfamiliar, but that their perception is actively pulled toward a single category?

Question two. Turkish has many more word endings than Spanish, yet many linguists would say Turkish morphology is in some ways easier to learn. Why? What is the difference between agglutinative and fusional systems that explains it?

Question three. English uses fairly rigid word order, while Russian and Finnish allow much freer order. Explain the connection to case marking, and why the loss of case endings in English history made fixed word order more necessary.

Question four. Greenberg found that verb-final languages tend to use postpositions and put relative clauses before the noun. What is the processing explanation for why these features travel together?

Question five. Why does comprehension fall off sharply when you know ninety percent of the words in a text, rather than declining gently? And why does Swain's work with immersion students suggest that input alone is not enough?
