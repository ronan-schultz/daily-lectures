Daily Lecture, afternoon edition. Kahneman and Tversky: what they found, and what survived.

In the spring of nineteen sixty-nine, a young psychologist named Amos Tversky gave a guest talk in a seminar at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. The seminar belonged to his colleague Daniel Kahneman. Tversky presented research suggesting that people, when they update their beliefs on new evidence, behave roughly like intuitive statisticians. They're a bit conservative, the research said, but basically Bayesian. Kahneman thought this was nonsense, and said so. He had spent years teaching statistics, and he had watched trained people, including himself, make the same intuitive mistakes over and over.

That argument started one of the most productive partnerships in the history of social science. Over the next decade and a half, the two of them took apart the assumption that human judgment is roughly rational, and replaced it with a map of the specific, predictable ways it goes wrong. Their work created behavioral economics, earned a Nobel Prize, and changed how governments, doctors, and investors think about decisions.

Then, near the end of Kahneman's life, part of the field built on top of their work collapsed in the replication crisis. Kahneman himself had to publicly admit that a chapter of his famous book rested on studies that didn't hold up.

So today has four parts. First, the two men, and why the partnership worked. Second, the core findings, the heuristics and biases. Third, prospect theory, the idea that won the Nobel. And fourth, the replication crisis: what broke, what survived, and how to tell the difference. At the end, five questions.

Part one. Two very different people.

Daniel Kahneman was born in nineteen thirty-four and spent his childhood in Paris. He was a Jewish boy in occupied France, and his family survived the war by hiding and moving. He has described himself as a natural worrier, a skeptic, someone who always assumed he was probably wrong. He emigrated to what became Israel, studied psychology, and as a young officer in the Israeli army he was given a remarkable job. He was asked to fix how the army evaluated recruits for officer training.

That early job matters, because it planted two ideas that run through everything he did later. The first was what he called the illusion of validity. Kahneman and his colleagues would watch candidates in a group exercise and feel completely confident about which ones would make good leaders. Then the feedback came back from officer school, and their predictions were barely better than chance. The striking thing was that knowing this did not reduce their confidence the next time. The feeling of certainty was generated by how coherent the story seemed, not by how accurate it was.

The second idea was that structure beats intuition. Kahneman designed an interview where the interviewer scored specific traits one at a time, using factual questions, and only then formed an overall impression. That structured method predicted better than the free-form interviews it replaced.

Amos Tversky was born in nineteen thirty-seven in Haifa. He was everything Kahneman was not. He was confident, funny, quick, and famously decisive. He had been a paratrooper and was decorated for bravery. Academically, he was a mathematical psychologist, trained in formal models of choice and measurement. People who knew him liked to say that the faster you realized Tversky was smarter than you, the smarter you were.

The partnership worked because it combined Kahneman's instinct for where people go wrong with Tversky's skill at turning a vague intuition into a clean, testable claim. They worked by talking, sometimes for hours a day, often at a single typewriter, and they deliberately let their authorship order alternate. They described the experience as sharing a mind.

Their method was unusual too. Instead of elaborate lab experiments, they mostly wrote short questions, tried them on themselves first, and then gave them to students and to experts. If the two of them felt the pull of the wrong answer, they assumed everyone else would too. And the experts were the point. Their very first paper together, in nineteen seventy-one, showed that research psychologists, people who taught statistics for a living, put far too much faith in small samples. They called it the belief in the law of small numbers. The idea is that people expect a small sample to look like the whole population, which the law of large numbers only promises for large ones.

Part two. Heuristics and biases.

In nineteen seventy-four they published a paper in the journal Science called Judgment under Uncertainty, Heuristics and Biases. The core idea was simple. When people face a hard question, like how probable something is, they quietly substitute an easier question, and answer that instead. These shortcuts, the heuristics, usually work fine. But because they're shortcuts, they fail in systematic, predictable directions, and those failures are the biases.

The paper named three heuristics.

The first is representativeness. To judge how likely something belongs to a category, people ask how much it resembles the typical member of that category. The famous demonstration came later, in nineteen eighty-three, and it's usually called the Linda problem. Participants read a description of Linda: thirty-one, single, outspoken, very bright, majored in philosophy, deeply concerned with discrimination and social justice. Then they're asked which is more probable. Option one, Linda is a bank teller. Option two, Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement.

Most people pick the second. But that's mathematically impossible. The probability of two things both being true can never exceed the probability of one of them. Every feminist bank teller is a bank teller. People were judging which description resembled Linda more, not which was more probable. This is called the conjunction fallacy. And here's the part that should bother you: adding detail to a story makes it feel more likely while making it strictly less likely. Every forecast, business plan, and investment thesis built from a chain of plausible steps has this problem.

The second heuristic is availability. People judge how frequent or likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. That usually correlates with actual frequency, but it also correlates with how vivid, recent, or heavily covered something is. So people overestimate the risk of dramatic causes of death, like plane crashes and shark attacks, and underestimate quiet ones, like diabetes.

The third is anchoring and adjustment. When people estimate a number, they start from whatever number is in front of them and adjust, but they adjust too little. The classic demonstration used a wheel of fortune that was rigged to stop at either ten or sixty-five. Participants spun it, then were asked whether the percentage of African countries in the United Nations was higher or lower than that number, and then to give their own estimate. The number on the wheel was obviously random. Yet the group that saw ten gave a median estimate of around twenty-five percent, and the group that saw sixty-five gave around forty-five percent. An irrelevant number pulled real estimates by twenty percentage points.

One more finding from this era is worth knowing, because it's about statistics itself. Kahneman told a story about teaching flight instructors in the Israeli air force. He was explaining that rewarding good performance works better than punishing bad performance. One instructor objected. In his experience, when he praised a cadet for an excellent maneuver, the next attempt was usually worse. When he screamed at a cadet for a terrible one, the next attempt was usually better. So, he concluded, punishment works and praise doesn't.

Kahneman realized the instructor was looking at regression to the mean. Any single performance is part skill and part luck. An unusually great maneuver probably involved some good luck, so the next one will tend to be closer to average, regardless of what the instructor says. Same for a terrible one. The instructor was attributing a statistical inevitability to his own feedback. And because the world tends to punish us for rewarding others and reward us for punishing them, this mistake teaches people exactly the wrong lesson. If you work with noisy performance data, this is the bias to watch for in yourself.

Part three. Prospect theory.

In the mid-seventies, Kahneman and Tversky turned from judgment, meaning how people estimate probabilities, to choice, meaning how people decide between risky options. The standard model was expected utility theory. You value an outcome by how it changes your total wealth, and you weigh each outcome by its probability. Their nineteen seventy-nine paper in Econometrica, called Prospect Theory, an Analysis of Decision under Risk, proposed a different description of what people actually do. It has four main ingredients.

The first is reference dependence. People don't evaluate outcomes in terms of total wealth. They evaluate them as gains or losses relative to a reference point, usually the status quo, sometimes an expectation. Getting a five thousand dollar raise feels great if you expected nothing and terrible if you expected ten thousand.

The second is loss aversion. Losses hurt more than equivalent gains feel good. The usual estimate is that a loss weighs roughly twice as much as a gain of the same size. That's why many people will refuse a coin flip that loses a hundred dollars on tails and wins a hundred and fifty on heads, even though the bet has positive expected value.

The third is diminishing sensitivity. The difference between zero and a hundred dollars feels bigger than the difference between eleven hundred and twelve hundred. That holds for losses too. The result is that people tend to be risk-averse for gains, preferring a sure thing, and risk-seeking for losses, preferring to gamble to avoid a sure loss. That second half explains a lot of bad behavior, from gamblers chasing losses to managers doubling down on failing projects.

The fourth is probability weighting. People don't weigh probabilities linearly. They overweight small probabilities, which helps explain why the same person buys both lottery tickets and insurance. And they underweight outcomes that are probable but not certain, compared with outcomes that are certain. The jump from ninety-nine percent to one hundred percent feels enormous, much bigger than from fifty to fifty-one.

The framing effect is where these ingredients become vivid. In a nineteen eighty-one paper, they posed what became known as the Asian disease problem. A disease outbreak is expected to kill six hundred people, and you have to choose between two programs. In one version, the options are described as lives saved. Program A will save two hundred people for sure. Program B has a one-third chance of saving all six hundred and a two-thirds chance of saving no one. About seventy-two percent chose the sure thing.

In the other version, the options are mathematically identical but described as deaths. Program C means four hundred people will die for sure. Program D has a one-third chance that nobody dies and a two-thirds chance that all six hundred die. Now about seventy-eight percent chose the gamble.

Same outcomes, different description, opposite preference. That's a direct problem for the standard rational model, which assumes preferences don't depend on how options are worded. Prospect theory predicts it exactly. Saved lives are framed as gains, so people are risk-averse. Deaths are framed as losses, so people are risk-seeking.

This work, together with the economist Richard Thaler, launched behavioral economics. It also changed policy. The idea of choice architecture, like making retirement savings the default instead of an opt-in, grows directly out of reference dependence and status quo effects.

Tversky did not live to see the full impact. He died of cancer in nineteen ninety-six, at fifty-nine. In two thousand two, Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in economics, and he made clear it was work they had done together. The Nobel isn't awarded posthumously, so Tversky could not share it.

Part four. The replication crisis, and what survived.

In two thousand eleven, Kahneman published Thinking, Fast and Slow. It became a huge bestseller, and it popularized a framing he borrowed from other psychologists: System One and System Two. System One is fast, automatic, and intuitive. System Two is slow, effortful, and deliberate. It's a useful metaphor, and Kahneman was always careful to say these are not literal brain systems.

But the book also leaned on a wider literature, beyond his own work, and some of it was about to fall apart.

Around the same time, psychology started to discover that many of its published findings didn't replicate. When independent teams reran classic studies with larger samples and preregistered plans, a large share of the effects shrank dramatically or disappeared. The causes are mostly statistical, and you'll recognize them. Studies with small samples and noisy measurements, combined with flexible analysis choices and a publishing system that rewards significant results, will produce a literature full of false positives and inflated effect sizes. It's the same mechanism as a backtest overfit by trying many specifications and reporting the best one.

The hardest-hit area was social priming. That's the idea that subtle cues can unconsciously change behavior in big ways. The most famous example was a nineteen ninety-six study claiming that people who unscrambled sentences with words related to old age, like wrinkle and Florida, afterwards walked more slowly down a hallway. Kahneman discussed this kind of priming in his book and wrote that disbelief was not an option. The result was real, he said, and readers had to accept it.

In two thousand twelve, a team re-ran the slow-walking study with better controls, including timing with infrared sensors instead of a stopwatch held by a research assistant. The effect didn't show up, except when the experimenters themselves expected it to. That same year, Kahneman wrote an open letter to priming researchers, warning of a train wreck looming and urging them to organize replications of their own work. Few did, and later analyses suggested much of that literature was unreliable.

A few years later, critics did a statistical audit of the priming chapter in his book. They looked at the sample sizes and the pattern of published results and concluded the evidence was much weaker than it looked. Kahneman responded publicly. He agreed. He said he had placed too much faith in underpowered studies, and that the critics were right to warn readers away from that chapter.

Another concept he discussed, ego depletion, also took a hit. That's the idea that willpower is a limited resource that gets used up, so exerting self-control on one task makes you worse at the next one. A large multi-lab replication in twenty sixteen found an effect close to zero.

So what survived? This is the important part, and it's more reassuring than the headlines. Large coordinated replication projects retested many classic findings across dozens of labs. Anchoring replicated, and strongly. Framing effects like the Asian disease problem replicated. A twenty twenty project retested the original prospect theory questions in nineteen countries, and the core pattern held up well. Loss aversion is debated more seriously. Some researchers argue it's smaller or more context-dependent than the famous two-to-one ratio suggests, especially for small stakes. But the broader idea that people evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point is about as solid as anything in psychology.

Notice the pattern. What held up were findings from simple, within-person demonstrations with large effects that are easy to rerun: here's a question, most people get it wrong in a predictable direction. What fell apart were small studies claiming large behavioral effects from subtle manipulations. You could have predicted which was which from the statistics alone. That's Kahneman and Tversky's first paper, about the law of small numbers, coming back around. The field had been believing in small samples, exactly the bias they'd identified in nineteen seventy-one.

There's one more piece of Kahneman's legacy worth taking home. Late in his career, he championed what he called adversarial collaboration. Instead of trading critical papers with people who disagree with you, you design a study together in advance and agree on what result would settle the question. His best-known example was with Gary Klein, a researcher who studied expert intuition in firefighters and nurses and was much more optimistic about intuition than Kahneman. Their two thousand nine paper landed on a clean answer. Expert intuition can be trusted when two conditions hold. The environment must be regular enough to be learnable, and the expert must have had lots of practice with quick, clear feedback. Chess players and firefighters qualify. Long-range political forecasters and many stock pickers don't, because their environments are noisy and the feedback is slow or ambiguous.

Kahneman died in two thousand twenty-four, at ninety.

Let's recap.

Kahneman and Tversky showed that human judgment departs from rationality in specific, predictable directions. People substitute easy questions for hard ones: resemblance for probability, ease of recall for frequency, and an arbitrary starting point for a real estimate. Prospect theory replaced total wealth with gains and losses from a reference point, losses weighing about twice as much, diminishing sensitivity in both directions, and distorted weights on probabilities. That explains why the same choice gets opposite answers depending on how it's framed. The replication crisis knocked down much of the social priming literature and ego depletion, and Kahneman admitted he had trusted underpowered studies. But the core heuristics, framing, and reference dependence held up across large multi-lab replications. The underlying lesson is the one they started with: small samples lie, and confidence is not evidence.
